Working in the Gulf: Sleep, Heat, Long Shifts and Loneliness

By Kathmandu Mental Health ClinicPublished 21 October 20264 min readHow we write our guides

Long shifts in the heat, little sleep, money worry and a long way from family. Working in the Gulf takes a toll that many workers carry in silence. Here is what is normal, what is not, and where to get help.

Key takeaways

  • Heat, shift work, debt and distance from family strain sleep and mood. Feeling it is normal.
  • Protect sleep first: fixed wake time, no caffeine late, no alcohol or unprescribed tablets.
  • Low mood lasting more than two weeks, heavy drinking, or thoughts of self-harm mean it is time to speak to a doctor.
  • You can see a Nepali-speaking psychiatrist online from the Gulf.

Why the Gulf is hard on the mind

Many Nepali workers in Qatar, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Oman and Bahrain do heavy, repetitive work far from family. Heat, long or night shifts, shared rooms, debt and being away from children and parents add up. It is normal to feel the strain. It is not a personal failure.

Sleep: the first thing to go

Shift changes, noisy shared rooms, extreme heat and worry about money all break sleep. Poor sleep then worsens mood, temper and concentration, and raises the risk of accidents at work.

  • Keep a fixed wake time, even on days off.
  • Use a cover over the eyes or earplugs in a crowded room.
  • Cut tea, coffee and energy drinks six hours before sleep.
  • Avoid alcohol and unprescribed sleeping tablets to "switch off".

See our guide to insomnia treatment.

Loneliness and low mood

Missing a child's first steps or a parent's illness hurts. Over months, homesickness can turn into depression: low mood most of the day, no interest in anything, tiredness, hopeless thoughts, drinking more, or pulling away from friends.

Short daily video calls home, a regular meet-up with workmates, prayer or a religious group, and physical activity all protect mood. If low mood lasts more than two weeks, speak to a doctor. See depression signs and treatment.

Money worry, anger and overthinking

Loans, unpaid wages, remittance pressure and family demands fuel constant worry. Racing thoughts at night, a pounding heart and tension are signs of anxiety, which is treatable. Read anxiety treatment and medicine and overthinking: when to get help.

Warning signs not to ignore

  • Thoughts of ending your life or feeling everyone would be better off without you.
  • Hearing voices, strong suspicion or fear that others cannot see.
  • Drinking or taking substances every day to cope.
  • Not eating, not sleeping for several days, or being unable to work.

If you are in danger, contact your local emergency number straight away. In Nepal, call the suicide prevention helpline 1166. Tell a trusted friend or family member where you are and how you feel.

Getting help from abroad

You do not have to wait until you are home. Our psychiatrists consult by video in Nepali, so you can talk about how you feel in your own language, in confidence, without travelling. A family member in Nepal can help with booking if you wish. If you need medicine, we explain the options and, where appropriate, coordinate with a local doctor, since prescribing follows the rules of the country you live in. Choose your country: Qatar, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Oman, Bahrain, or see all countries.

Frequently asked questions

Can I see a psychiatrist online from the Gulf?

Yes. Our doctors consult by video in Nepali or English for Nepalis living abroad. Pick your country on our Nepalis abroad page for details.

Will my employer or family know?

No. Your information is kept confidential and shared only with your consent, except where there is a serious risk to life.

Is feeling homesick a mental illness?

No. Homesickness is natural. It becomes a concern when low mood, poor sleep or worry last for weeks and affect work or relationships.
