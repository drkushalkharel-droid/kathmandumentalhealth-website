Depression and family support

How to Support Someone With Depression

Kathmandu Mental Health ClinicMedically reviewed by Dr. Kushal Kharel, Consultant Psychiatrist · Online therapyUpdated 27 September 2026

When someone you care about is depressed, it can be hard to know what to say or do. You do not need to fix everything. Listening, staying connected, and helping with one manageable next step can make it easier for them to seek care.

Understand what depression can look like

Depression can affect mood, interest, energy, sleep, appetite, concentration, and everyday tasks. A person may seem sad, irritable, withdrawn, or unusually tired. Symptoms can affect relationships, study, work, and home life. Only a qualified professional can assess what is happening; do not try to diagnose someone from a checklist.

Depression is treatable. Care may involve psychological treatment, medicine, or both, depending on the person’s needs and clinical assessment. WHO: depressive disorder (depression).

Ways you can offer support

  • Listen with patience. Try, “I’m glad you told me,” or “Would you like me to listen, or help think through one next step?”
  • Keep invitations simple. Offer a short walk, a meal, or sitting together. If they decline, keep the door open without pressure.
  • Offer practical help. Ask whether they want help finding a clinician, arranging transport, making an appointment, or handling one everyday task.
  • Stay in touch. A brief, kind check-in can matter. Avoid making your support conditional on them seeming better.
  • Look after your own limits. Supporting someone can be tiring. Seek support for yourself too, while respecting their privacy.

What to avoid

Comments such as “just cheer up,” “others have it worse,” or “you have nothing to be sad about” can make someone feel blamed or misunderstood. Try not to argue about whether their feelings are reasonable, promise that everything will be fine, or pressure them to stop treatment. Ask what kind of support feels useful to them.

If you are worried about suicide or immediate safety

Take talk of suicide seriously. Ask directly and calmly whether they are thinking about suicide or hurting themselves. Stay with them if there is immediate danger, involve a trusted person, and get urgent professional help. Do not promise to keep an immediate safety risk secret.

If danger is immediate, contact local emergency services or go to an emergency department. In Nepal, call Police 100, Ambulance 102, or the suicide prevention helpline 1166. Elsewhere, find a local crisis line at Find A Helpline. WHO advises helping a person in acute distress connect with a health worker or counsellor. WHO: suicide questions and answers.
