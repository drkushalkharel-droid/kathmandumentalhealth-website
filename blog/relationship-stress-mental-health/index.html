Relationships and wellbeing

Relationship Stress and Mental Health: What Can Help

Kathmandu Mental Health ClinicMedically reviewed by Dr. Kushal Kharel, Consultant Psychiatrist · Online therapyUpdated 27 September 2026

Arguments, distance, money worries, family responsibilities, or a major life change can put pressure on a relationship. Ongoing conflict can affect sleep and mood, while anxiety or depression can also make communication harder. Recognizing the strain is a useful first step; it does not by itself explain the whole relationship or decide what you should do.

How relationship stress can affect wellbeing

People respond to conflict in different ways. Some become quiet or avoid conversations; others feel tense, irritable, worried, or unable to sleep. These experiences can have many causes. If low mood, anxiety, sleep problems, or loss of interest persist or interfere with everyday life, consider speaking with a health professional rather than assuming the relationship is the only cause.

Likewise, mental health concerns can change how partners connect. A person who is exhausted or depressed may withdraw; someone who is very anxious may need more reassurance. This can be difficult for both people, and support may involve care for one person, the relationship, or both.

Small steps for a difficult conversation

  • Choose a time when neither of you is rushing or already very upset.
  • Talk about one recent, specific issue instead of listing every past disagreement.
  • Use statements about your own experience: “I feel alone when we do not talk after work,” rather than accusations.
  • Pause if the discussion escalates. Agree on when to return to it, if it is safe to do so.
  • Ask what the other person understood before trying to solve the problem.

These ideas are not a substitute for counselling and will not fit every situation. You are not responsible for making another person change.

When counselling or individual support may help

Consider seeking support if the same conflict repeats without resolution, communication has turned into ongoing criticism or silence, trust feels difficult to rebuild, or relationship stress is affecting sleep, work, study, or mood. Couples counselling may help when both people can participate safely and freely. One partner can also seek individual support if the other is not ready.

A clinician can help explore whether stress is connected with depression, anxiety, substance use, sleep difficulties, or another concern. Mental health care should be based on an individual assessment, not an assumption about who is to blame.

Safety comes first

Joint counselling may not be appropriate when someone is afraid, being threatened, controlled, or harmed. If there is violence or coercion, prioritize safety and contact trusted local support or emergency services. Do not use a relationship conversation or online article in place of a safety plan.

If you are in immediate danger, move to a safer place if possible and contact local emergency services. For mental health emergencies in Nepal, call Police 100, Ambulance 102, or suicide prevention helpline 1166.
