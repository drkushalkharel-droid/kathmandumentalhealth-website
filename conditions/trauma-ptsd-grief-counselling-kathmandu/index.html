Trauma and grief

Trauma, PTSD and Grief in Kathmandu: Signs, Treatment and Support

By Kathmandu Mental Health ClinicPublished 21 October 20264 min readHow we write our guides

A shock, an accident, violence or the death of someone close can leave memories and feelings that will not settle. These reactions are understandable, they are not weakness, and trauma and grief respond well to the right support.

Key takeaways

  • Upsetting memories after a shock are common. If they last beyond about a month or disrupt daily life, treatment helps.
  • PTSD often hides behind poor sleep, anger, drinking or low mood.
  • Trauma-focused therapy and, where useful, medicine are effective. You do not have to relive everything at once.
  • Grief is not an illness, but prolonged grief and depression can be treated.

Signs that trauma is still affecting you

  • Re-living: flashbacks, nightmares or sudden vivid memories, with a racing heart or sweating when something reminds you.
  • Avoiding: staying away from places, people, news or conversations linked to what happened.
  • Low mood and guilt: feeling numb, detached, ashamed or blaming yourself.
  • On edge: poor sleep, irritability, anger, being easily startled, trouble concentrating.

In Nepali: आघात, पोस्ट-ट्रमाटिक स्ट्रेस, शोक. Many people in Nepal carry memories of the 2015 earthquake, road accidents, violence, the loss of a family member, or hard experiences at work abroad.

Normal reaction, PTSD or acute stress?

After a frightening event, shock, poor sleep and upsetting memories are common and often ease within a few weeks. When symptoms last beyond about a month, or are getting in the way of work, study or family life, it may be post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Trauma can also appear as depression, anxiety, panic or heavy drinking, so the cause is not always obvious.

Grief that does not ease

Grief after a death is natural and has no fixed timetable. Most people slowly find a way to live with the loss. If, a year or more later, the longing is still intense, daily life is on hold, or you cannot accept the loss, this can be prolonged grief, which responds well to specialist support. Deep sadness, loss of interest, hopelessness or thoughts of ending your life are signs to get help sooner, not later.

What can help at home

  • Keep a steady routine for sleep, meals and some daily movement.
  • Talk to someone you trust, at your own pace. You do not have to retell everything.
  • Use grounding when memories flood in: feel your feet on the floor, name five things you can see, breathe out slowly.
  • Go easy on alcohol and sleeping pills. They can make nights and mood worse.
  • Try our free breathing exercise and feelings wheel.

Treatment

  • Trauma-focused therapy: structured CBT-based therapy that helps you process the memory, face avoided situations step by step and change guilt-laden beliefs. It is among the treatments with the strongest evidence for PTSD.
  • Medicine: some antidepressants (SSRIs) can ease PTSD, depression and anxiety. A psychiatrist can also treat sleep problems safely, without relying on long-term sedatives.
  • Grief support: counselling that makes space for the loss and helps you rebuild meaning and routine, with attention to depression where it is present.
  • Family: with your consent, relatives can learn how to help without pressure.

When to get help

See a psychiatrist if symptoms last more than a month, if you are drinking or using substances to cope, if you cannot sleep, or if you are having thoughts of self-harm. If you are in danger right now, call Police 100, Ambulance 102 or the suicide prevention helpline 1166.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to talk about everything that happened?

No. Good trauma care is paced with you. You choose how much to share, and we first build safety and coping skills.

Can PTSD appear years later?

Yes. Symptoms sometimes start months or years after the event, often triggered by stress, an anniversary or a reminder.

Is grief the same as depression?

No, but they can overlap. If low mood, hopelessness or loss of interest spreads into everything and does not lift, depression may need separate treatment.

Will I need medicine?

Not always. Therapy is the first choice for many people. Medicine is considered when symptoms are severe, when there is depression or sleep loss, or when therapy alone is not enough.
