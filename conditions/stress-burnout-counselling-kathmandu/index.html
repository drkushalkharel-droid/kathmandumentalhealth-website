Stress and burnout

Stress, Burnout and Exam Pressure in Kathmandu: Signs and How to Recover

By Kathmandu Mental Health ClinicPublished 25 September 20263 min readHow we write our guides

Long hours, heavy workloads, exam pressure, family expectations and money worries leave many people in Kathmandu exhausted. Some stress is normal, but when it becomes constant it wears down body and mind. This page explains how to tell stress from burnout, what helps, and when to get professional support.

Key takeaways

  • Stress is a response to pressure. Burnout is prolonged, unmanaged work stress that leaves you exhausted, cynical and less effective.
  • Long-term stress can lead to anxiety, depression, poor sleep and physical illness.
  • Rest, boundaries, sleep, talking to someone and practical problem-solving help, and counselling speeds recovery.
  • See a doctor if you cannot cope, if sleep or mood are affected, or if you use alcohol or tablets to manage.

Stress and burnout: the difference

Stress is your body's reaction to demands, and usually eases when the pressure ends. Burnout, as described by the World Health Organization, is an occupational phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It has three features: exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism about work, and reduced effectiveness. Burnout is not itself a medical illness, but it can lead to depression and anxiety.

Warning signs

  • Constant tiredness that rest does not fix, and trouble sleeping.
  • Irritability, feeling overwhelmed, or dreading work or study.
  • Headaches, muscle tension, stomach problems, frequent illness.
  • Difficulty concentrating, forgetfulness, and lower performance.
  • Withdrawing from people, or relying on caffeine, alcohol, tobacco or tablets to cope.

Common causes here

Heavy workloads and long hours, job insecurity, exam and board pressure, competition, expectations from family, caring for elders and children together, money worries, and, for many, the strain of working or studying far from home.

What helps

  • Protect sleep, and take real breaks, meals and time away from screens.
  • Set boundaries: say no where you can, and share tasks.
  • Break problems into steps and tackle the most important first.
  • Move your body daily and practise slow breathing.
  • Talk to someone you trust, and do not carry it alone.
  • Cut back on caffeine and avoid alcohol or sedatives as coping tools.

For students: exam pressure

Plan study in short blocks with breaks, sleep properly, especially before exams, and avoid comparing yourself with classmates. Anxiety before exams is common, and a counsellor or doctor can help if it affects sleep, concentration or health. Parents can help most with calm support rather than pressure.

When to seek help

Get support when stress lasts more than a few weeks, affects sleep or mood, or leads to panic, hopelessness or thoughts of self-harm. A psychiatrist can check whether stress has become anxiety or depression and treat it with counselling, CBT and, when needed, medicine. See anxiety treatment and depression.

Frequently asked questions

Is burnout a mental illness?

The World Health Organization classes burnout as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition. It can, however, lead to depression and anxiety, which are treatable.

How long does it take to recover from burnout?

It varies from weeks to months. Recovery is faster when the causes at work or home are addressed as well as the symptoms, and when you have rest and support.

How do I know if it is stress or depression?

Stress usually eases when the pressure ends. If low mood, loss of interest, hopelessness or sleep and appetite changes continue for two weeks or more, see a doctor.

Can exam stress be treated?

Yes. Study planning, sleep, relaxation skills and counselling help, and a doctor can treat severe anxiety or sleep problems around exams.
